Philippians

Chained in a Roman prison, Paul writes the most joyful letter in the New Testament, urging a beloved church to rejoice always and to think like the Christ who emptied himself.

Joy From a Prison Cell

Writing to thank a generous, affectionate church for their support, Paul models contentment in every circumstance and calls believers to the same humility that led Christ from glory to the cross.

What happens in Philippians

Paul writes warmly to a church that has partnered with him in the gospel from the beginning, expressing confidence that even his imprisonment has served to advance the gospel by emboldening other believers to preach Christ. He urges the Philippians toward unity and humility, grounding the appeal in the supreme example of Christ, who though equal with God emptied himself, took the form of a servant, and humbled himself to death on a cross, for which God has highly exalted him above every name. Paul holds up Timothy and Epaphroditus as examples of selfless service, then warns against those who trust in religious credentials, counting his own impressive pedigree as rubbish compared to knowing Christ and being found in him. He urges two women in the church to be reconciled, calls the Philippians to rejoice in the Lord always, and commends whatever is true, honest, just, pure, lovely, and of good report as the proper focus of a believer's mind. The letter closes with Paul's testimony that he has learned to be content whether abased or abounding, because he can do all things through Christ who strengthens him, along with thanks for their financial gift.

Why it matters

Philippians offers the clearest description in the New Testament of Christ's self-emptying humility, a pattern that has shaped Christian understanding of servanthood ever since, and its repeated call to rejoice regardless of circumstance has made it a source of comfort for believers facing hardship across every generation.

“I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me.” — Philippians 4:13
