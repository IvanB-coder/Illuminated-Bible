What happens in Genesis

Genesis does not ease you in — it opens with the universe itself catching fire into being. Darkness gives way to light, water parts from sky, and a garden blooms around two people who are told, simply, that they are loved and trusted. That trust cracks in a single bite, and the rest of the book is the long, honest record of what a broken world does next: a flood that judges the earth, a tower built out of human pride, and then — almost quietly, almost like an afterthought — a call to one man, Abraham, to walk toward a land and a future he cannot see. From there the story narrows into a family saga: a barren wife who laughs at an impossible promise, brothers who wrestle in the womb and later wrestle with God Himself, a coat of many colours that becomes the hinge of a nation's survival. By the final page, seventy descendants of Abraham are living in Egypt, fed by the very brother they once sold into slavery.

Why it matters

Every thread the Bible later pulls on is first tied here. The dignity of being made in God's image. The weight of choice. The stubbornness of grace that keeps choosing people who keep disappointing it. Genesis ends not with a victory parade but with a coffin in Egypt and a promise still unpaid — a cliffhanger that the rest of Scripture spends sixty-five more books answering.

“In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth.” — Genesis 1:1
